Councilmember Dilg's statement.

I want to start by acknowledging that I voted in favor of entering into this contract originally. And I also want to acknowledge that, looking back, I regret that vote.

At the time, my focus was on making sure our police department had access to the best tools and technology available to help them do their jobs and keep our community safe. I have tremendous respect for our Chief and for the men and women of our police department, and I trusted — and continue to trust — their intentions in using this technology.

When Councilmember Bogert raised concerns about Flock and the privacy implications of this technology, it was honestly the first time I had heard many of those concerns. I had not done enough research into the experiences of other communities, the broader privacy questions surrounding license plate reader technology, or the ways this data could potentially be accessed and used beyond our own police department.

I should have. That was my responsibility as a councilmember, and I apologize to our residents for not doing that due diligence before casting my vote.

Since then, I have spent considerably more time looking at these issues, and what I have learned has changed my position.

My concern is not primarily about how our own police department intends to use this technology. My concern is about what happens to this information once it exists.

There have been troubling examples and allegations around the country involving the sharing and use of license plate reader data, including its use to locate or track people who were not suspected of committing a crime. That should concern all of us.

And even if we have complete confidence in our own Chief and officers — and I do have tremendous confidence in them — we cannot guarantee the judgment, policies, ethics, or future leadership of every outside agency that may have access to information originating in our community.

We also cannot know what state or federal governments may require in the future.

That is particularly troubling to me. We can establish policies today about retention, access, and appropriate use. But if state or federal law changes, or if we receive a lawful order requiring this data to be turned over, our ability as a city to say no may be extremely limited. Future laws could require data to be retained longer or used in ways that this council and our residents would never agree to today.

Once this type of surveillance infrastructure exists, we cannot guarantee that the rules governing it will always remain the rules we agreed to when we installed it.

But there is another consideration that, for me, carries just as much weight.

We are a representative government.

The people sitting at this table were elected to represent the people who live here. And since this issue has come forward, I have heard from an extraordinary number of residents asking us to end this contract.

And this has not been a partisan issue.

I have heard concerns from Republicans, Democrats, independents, libertarians, and people who probably disagree with one another about almost everything else. Their reasons may differ, but the message has

been remarkably consistent: they do not want this technology monitoring vehicles traveling through their community.

Some may characterize changing my vote because of that public response as folding to political pressure. They are certainly free to call it that.

I call it listening.

The people of this community put me in this chair and trusted me to represent them honestly. Representation does not mean listening only when residents agree with the decision I have already made. Sometimes it means being willing to reconsider that decision.

That is where I am tonight.

It is a judgment about this particular technology, the collection and sharing of this particular kind of data, and whether its continued use reflects the wishes and values of the community we were elected to represent.

For those reasons, I will be voting to discontinue our contract with Flock and to provide the required 30-day notice.
