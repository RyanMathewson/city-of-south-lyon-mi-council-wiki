## Rules of Conduct for Public Comment at Council Meetings $ ^{*} $

Members of the public may speak at a Council meeting upon recognition by the Mayor. Public comment may only occur during periods designated on the agenda for public comment or a public hearing. A person may speak for up to two (2) minutes during each of the two public comment periods on agenda items or non-agenda items. Waivers of the time requirement may only be granted in the discretion of the Mayor, and waivers to speak at a time other than a designated comment period may only be granted by the Council. Any person wishing to make a presentation longer than two minutes or requiring audio-visual equipment is asked to contact the City Clerk requesting to appear on a future agenda.

A person may only address Council from the podium. Only one person may occupy the podium at a time. All remarks are to be directed to the Mayor and Council. Speakers are not to engage in direct dialog with other meeting attendees.

Any person who violates the Rules of Conduct, disturbs the peace at the meeting, and/or interferes with the meeting may be warned, ordered to be seated, removed, and/or ticketed.

 $ ^{*} $This summarizes Council Resolution 04-18. Complete Rules, including guidelines for considering waivers, are available in the Council Chambers and from the City Clerk.

Suzann Martin, six zero two West Lake Street - Regarding the West Lake Planned Urban Development, only a handful of residents were notified about this project. Residents with homes within 50 feet of the property. If all the residents of the city were notified, there would be more in opposition to the wetland encroachments.

I am opposed to building on the wetlands - three homes and a boulevard are planned.

I inquired to Kris Olsson, Wetland Specialist and Watershed Ecologist, with the Huron River Watershed Council, about the ten acres of wetlands on this property owned by another party. We discussed the following important message:

“If there are feasible alternatives to going through the wetlands, the Huron River Watershed Council’s position is to maintain the Huron River Watershed’s health, to encourage higher density where built infrastructure already exists, and preserve natural areas where “Green Infrastructure” exists so it can continue to provide ecological services necessary to maintain quality of water, air, land, and life. Examples of green infrastructure are wetlands, forests, creeks, and lakes.

The Huron River Watershed Council has a general rule, the biggest focus is to make sure the development is built in a way that is protective of water quality and natural features. So, if a development is going in, they would recommend good storm water management and protection of existing wetlands and forests, so that polluted runoff does not affect waterways. Also important would be a setback from the creek or any other waterways or wetlands to protect those important features. Higher and taller density is better from a storm water perspective, since the amount of impervious surfaces created per household is minimized, yet accommodates more people, which would relieve development pressure on outlying rural areas. While development in a city is generally appropriate,  $ \underline{\text{it is never appropriate to destroy wetlands and other natural features like riparian buffers and mature forested areas}} $. Wetlands are especially important to protect in urban areas for their ability to soak up runoff and provide habitat to what wildlife has remained in the city."

Some of Kris Olsson's, and the Huron River Watershed Council, points were to avoid damage to any wetlands and preserve as many trees and as much of the natural topography as possible and group the built areas together to minimize paving and destruction of natural areas. Protect natural features like woods, wetlands, steep slopes, and waterways, and control and treat storm water.

# Services Provided to Member Governments

The Huron River Watershed Council is a public, nonprofit coalition of local governments, businesses, and citizens established to protect the Huron River and its tributary streams, lakes, wetlands, and groundwater. Membership is voluntary for municipalities located wholly or partially within the Huron River Watershed. Annual membership dues for townships, cities, and villages are assessed at a rate of $0.10 per capita (based on 2010 census figures). Annual dues for counties are assessed at a rate of $0.05 per capita (watershed population) plus $1.50 per square mile of watershed area. Each community is able to appoint one representative and an alternate to the Council for each 20,000 in population. For counties, representation is based upon the population of eligible communities not otherwise represented. The following are programs and services of the Council that communities receive for their membership.

### I. Water Quality Monitoring and Education

### A. Promote and Coordinate Studies Regarding Water Resources

1. HRWC staff, board members, and volunteers serve on local river/watershed related committees and work with lake associations, municipal agencies and departments as requested;

2. The Council performs fieldwork to generate water quality information (stream and river quality, too), and utilizes that and other existing water quality and quantity information to develop watershed management plans for priority areas of the Huron River Basin;

3. The Adopt-A-Stream program generates creek reports for the creeks and streams in the monitoring program.

### B. Prepare periodic reports concerning emerging issues within the Huron River Watershed

C. Provide specific information to the public regarding the Huron River, its lakes, streams, flood plains, wetlands and groundwater, and research conducted by the Council

### D. Distribute a quarterly newsletter on water resource issues

The newsletter informs members of Council activities and emerging issues regarding water quality and the Huron River. Copies are provided for each member of the community's governing board and planning commission. Additional copies are provided for distribution to the public visiting the municipal offices and through area public libraries. Additional action bulletins/emails are distributed when the need arises to detail important initiatives affecting the river.

### E. Conduct presentations, workshops, and hands-on educational programs on the Huron River and its ecosystem

1. We train teachers in the use of environmental education materials on rivers, wetlands, and groundwater, and develop new curricula to augment existing curricula;

2. The Council has developed the State's premiere Adopt-A-Stream program through which citizen volunteers receive hands-on training to learn stream ecology, assess the health of area creeks and take action locally to protect these sensitive systems;

3. Staff is available to do video and slide presentations.

### II. Technical Assistance and Policy Development

A. Provide technical analysis of data and reports regarding water resources

### B. Provide technical assistance in the development of water resource protection policy and ordinances

1. We assist local planners in stormwater management planning, and in developing ordinances/policies for protecting wetlands, flood plains, riparian zones and groundwater resources;

2. The Council also provides statewide leadership in issues related to watershed protection.

## Disposing of Yard Waste in the Off Season

We've had a few warm days these past couple of weeks, which means many of you may have started cleaning up your gardens and picking up brush. You've filled your paper yard waste bags, but curbside collection doesn't start until next month... Now what?

Yard waste can be composted in your own backyard!

Composting captures nutrients from decomposed leaves, grass, and other vegetative waste, and the resulting compost is a nutrient-rich soil amendment that will enhance your soil, yard, and garden come spring. Learn more about the benefits of at-home composting on our Composting page, as well as how to get started. Always check with your local DPW first to determine if your city has any regulations on composting!

*[Image or figure on this page; see the original.]*

*[Image or figure on this page; see the original.]*

Another way to dispose of your yard waste in the off season is by taking it to a composting facility such as Spurt Industries in Wixom. At a composting facility, your yard waste decomposes and becomes a rich, black humus that is used in gardens and landscaping projects all while being kept out of the landfill. Check locally to find a composting facility that accepts yard waste near you!

As consumers, you have the power to make a lasting change by taking direct action - that is, recycling right and making sustainable choices every day!
